# Propaganda, Persuasion and Your Brand: What Customers Actually See

Every founder knows their brand from the inside: the months spent on the formula, the supplier they fought for, the reason they started. It feels obvious why the product is better.

Customers don't see any of that. They see a two-second frame between a friend's holiday photo and a meme. Whatever they make of your brand, they make from what fits in that gap.

That gap, between how you feel about your brand and the picture customers carry of it, is the oldest problem in mass communication. People have been studying it for a hundred years, under a name that now sounds sinister: propaganda.

This isn't a guide to manipulating anyone. It is about understanding how perception actually forms, so the true story of your brand is the one that lands.

## The pictures in our heads

In 1922, the journalist Walter Lippmann wrote "Public Opinion", a book about why people misunderstand the world. His answer: nobody responds to the world directly. We respond to "the pictures in our heads", simplified images built from whatever fragments reached us.

Your customers do the same with your brand. They don't evaluate your product; they evaluate their picture of it. That picture is built from a handful of fragments: an ad, a package in a friend's bathroom, a review, a price.

**The lesson for founders:** you are not competing on what your product is. You are competing on the picture of it that forms in a stranger's head from very little information. Every ad is a fragment of that picture.

## Bernays and selling the meaning, not the object

Edward Bernays, Freud's nephew, published "Propaganda" in 1928 and is often called the father of public relations. His insight was that people rarely buy objects for what they are; they buy what the objects mean to them, such as status, safety, belonging or identity.

His campaigns were often ethically dubious, and the word "propaganda" has rightly come to describe deception. But the mechanism he described is real and neutral: **meaning is attached to products by stories, symbols and context, not by specifications.**

A D2C example: two identical water bottles. One is "a 750 ml steel bottle". The other is "the bottle you keep at your desk instead of buying plastic every day". Same object, different meaning, and the meaning is what gets bought.

**The lesson:** decide what your product means in the customer's life, and make every ad carry that meaning. If you don't choose a meaning, the customer will assign one, usually "just another one of those".

## Familiarity feels like truth

In 1968 the psychologist Robert Zajonc showed that people come to like things simply because they've seen them before. It's called the **mere-exposure effect**. Later research found a related effect: statements people have heard before feel more true, even when they aren't.

For brands, this is both a warning and an opportunity:

- **The warning:** one brilliant ad seen once does almost nothing. Familiarity is built by repetition.
- **The opportunity:** a small brand that shows up consistently, with the same colours, the same voice and the same kind of shot, slowly becomes the familiar choice, and familiar feels safe.

This is why the most important property of your ads isn't how clever each one is. It's how consistent they are with each other.

## Framing: the same fact, a different decision

In 1981, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman showed that people make different choices when the same facts are framed differently. "90% fat-free" and "10% fat" describe the same yoghurt; one sells.

Every ad frames. You choose:

- **gain or loss:** "glow in two weeks" or "stop wasting money on serums that don't work";
- **the comparison point:** ₹999 against a salon visit, or against a cheaper competitor;
- **what is in the frame at all:** the result, the routine, the ingredient, the person.

**The lesson:** honest framing isn't spin. It's choosing which true thing to say first. Choose deliberately, because the first frame decides how everything after it is read.

## Social proof: we look at each other

In "Influence" (1984), Robert Cialdini described **social proof**: when we're unsure, we copy what people like us are doing. For an unknown brand, uncertainty is the default state of every new visitor.

That's why the strongest D2C ads so often aren't about the product at all. They're about people like the viewer, using it and saying it works, in their own words.

## How brands actually grow: be easy to think of

The marketing scientist Byron Sharp, in "How Brands Grow" (2010), drew on decades of buying data to argue that brands grow mainly by being **easy to think of** and **easy to buy** for as many category buyers as possible. He calls it mental availability.

Two practical consequences:

- **Distinctive assets matter more than messages.** Colours, shapes, logos, characters and sounds that are yours and only yours let people recognise you in a fraction of a second, before they've processed any claim.
- **Reach beats intensity.** Being seen by many potential buyers regularly beats convincing a few deeply.

## Putting it together: closing the gap

The gap between how you feel about your brand and how customers see it doesn't close by explaining harder. It closes by building the right picture, one fragment at a time:

- **Choose the meaning:** what your product stands for in the customer's life, in one sentence.
- **Choose your distinctive assets:** a colour, a shot style, a voice, a character. Then never change them casually.
- **Repeat:** many consistent ads beat one brilliant one.
- **Frame honestly and deliberately:** pick the true thing that should land first.
- **Show people like the viewer:** proof from peers beats claims from you.
- **Never deceive.** Perception built on a false picture collapses at the first delivery, and the reviews remember.
