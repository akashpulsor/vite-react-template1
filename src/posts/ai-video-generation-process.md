# The AI Video Generation Process, Step by Step

Typing one prompt and getting a finished ad is not how usable AI video gets made.

A single prompt gives you a clip. A video that sells something, explains something, or tells a story is twenty or thirty clips that have to agree with each other: the same character, the same product, the same light, the same pace. That agreement doesn't come from the model. It comes from the process around it.

This is the process we run at Dalaillama for every client video, in the order it happens.

## 1. Brief: decide what the video has to do

Before anything is generated, write down the job of the video in one sentence, who it's for, where it will run, and how long it can be.

- **Job:** "Make a working parent believe this snack is cleaner than the usual packet, without preaching."
- **Audience:** who is watching, and what they already believe.
- **Placement:** Reels, YouTube Shorts, a website hero, a pitch deck. Each changes the aspect ratio, length and pacing.
- **Length:** a 15-second ad and a 60-second explainer are different productions, not one cut shorter.

Generation is the most expensive step in the whole pipeline. Every vague decision you leave for later turns into regenerated clips.

## 2. Script and screenplay

The script is what is said. The screenplay is what is seen while it is said.

Write them together. A line of voiceover that runs four seconds needs four seconds of picture under it, and that picture has to be something a model can actually produce. "She realises the brand cares" is not a shot. "Close-up: she reads the back of the pack, pauses, nods" is.

## 3. Shot plan: break the video into shots

Turn the screenplay into a numbered shot list. For each shot, decide:

- **Duration** in seconds.
- **Framing:** wide, medium, close-up, insert.
- **Camera movement:** static, slow push-in, pan, handheld.
- **Subject action:** what moves, and how much.
- **How the shot gets made.** This is the decision most teams skip. Not every shot should be generated by a video model. Some are better as motion graphics, some as a still image with a slow zoom, some as a product render.

That last column is where cost and quality are won or lost. We cover it in detail in [How to Reduce the Cost of AI Video Production](/blog/reduce-ai-video-production-cost).

## 4. Cast and references

Consistency across shots is the hardest problem in AI video. Solve it before generating anything:

- **Characters:** build one reference sheet per person (face, hair, wardrobe, from a few angles) and reuse it in every shot they appear in.
- **Product:** use real product photos as references, never a description of the product.
- **Look:** fix the colour palette, lighting style and lens feel once, and write them into every prompt the same way.

## 5. Keyframes: generate the image first

For each shot, generate a still image of its first frame (and sometimes its last) before generating any motion.

Images are far cheaper and faster than video. They are where you fix composition, character likeness, product accuracy and lighting. A client can approve twenty keyframes in one sitting. Asking them to approve twenty video clips means twenty times the generation spend on anything they reject.

Only an approved keyframe moves on to video.

## 6. Image-to-video generation

Each approved keyframe is animated with an image-to-video model, with a short motion prompt describing only what moves: "slow push-in, steam rises from the cup, she looks up at the camera".

A few rules that save the most retries:

- **Describe motion, not the scene.** The image already contains the scene.
- **Keep motion small and slow.** Big, fast movement is where models break hands, faces and products. You can add speed later in the edit.
- **Generate the shortest duration the shot needs.** A three-second shot doesn't need a ten-second generation.

## 7. Frame interpolation and retiming

Raw generations are often at a low frame rate, or slower than the final edit needs. Frame interpolation creates the in-between frames, so you can:

- raise a clip to a smooth 24, 30 or 60 fps;
- stretch a slow, stable generation into a longer slow-motion shot;
- speed-ramp a slow generation into a punchy moment without stutter.

## 8. Upscaling

Generate at the resolution you need to judge the shot, not the resolution you deliver at. Once the edit is locked, upscale only the clips that made the cut to 1080p or 4K.

## 9. Voice, music and sound

Record or generate the voiceover against the locked script. Choose music with the edit's pace, not after it. Add sound effects for every visible action: a pour, a click, a footstep. Sound does more to make AI footage feel real than any picture fix.

## 10. Edit, motion graphics and finish

Assemble the clips against the voiceover, add the motion graphics planned in step 3 (titles, product callouts, prices, end card), grade everything to one look, and export for each placement.

## 11. Review and delivery

Review the whole video, not the clips. Clients should comment on the cut, with each change tied to a shot number, so a fix means regenerating one shot and not the film.

## Where the process usually breaks

- **Skipping keyframes** and generating video straight from text, then paying for every rejected clip.
- **No shot plan**, so the edit discovers missing shots after the budget is gone.
- **No reference sheets**, so the main character changes face between shots.
- **Generating everything** when a third of the shots should have been motion graphics.
