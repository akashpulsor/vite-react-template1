# Ad Making Fundamentals for D2C Brands

Most D2C ads don't fail because they look cheap. They fail because nobody can tell what they're for.

A beautiful product shot with music and a logo is not an ad. It's a mood. An ad has a job: it moves a specific person one step closer to buying. Everything else, including the camera, the model, the colour grade and whether it was shot or generated with AI, serves that job or gets cut.

These are the fundamentals we see in every ad that works, whatever the category and whatever the budget.

## 1. One ad, one job

Before anything else, write the job in one sentence:

- "Make first-time visitors understand this serum is non-sticky."
- "Make people who abandoned their cart trust that delivery is fast."
- "Make existing customers try the new flavour."

An ad that tries to introduce the brand, explain three benefits, show reviews and announce a sale does none of them. If you have four messages, you have four ads.

## 2. One buyer, in one moment

"Women 25–40" is a targeting setting, not a buyer. Picture one person in one moment: scrolling on the metro at 8 a.m., tired, sceptical of skincare claims, has tried two serums that felt greasy.

Now every decision has an answer. What does she need to see in the first second? Greasy versus not greasy. What will she believe? A real texture shot, not a claim. How long will she give you? About two seconds before she scrolls on.

## 3. The hook is the first two seconds, not the first line

People decide whether to keep watching before they've read or heard anything. The opening frame has to work with the sound off and the caption unread.

Hooks that work for D2C:

- **The problem, shown:** the greasy fingertip, the tangled cable, the soggy snack.
- **The surprising result:** the product doing something the viewer didn't expect.
- **The pattern break:** a frame that doesn't look like an ad.
- **The direct question,** on screen in big text: "Still using a shampoo with sulphates?"

Don't open with your logo. Nobody stops scrolling for a logo they don't know yet.

## 4. Show product truth, not adjectives

"Lightweight", "premium" and "natural" are claims every competitor makes. Viewers have learned to ignore them.

Show the thing that makes the claim true:

- the serum absorbing in five seconds,
- the bag holding a laptop, a water bottle and lunch,
- the ingredient list, close up, with nothing hidden,
- the before and after, in the same light.

If you can't show it, the claim probably isn't strong enough to lead with.

## 5. Proof beats persuasion

A shopper who has never heard of you has one main question: "Is this a risk?" Proof lowers that risk faster than any line of copy:

- real customers using it, in their own words,
- numbers that mean something ("4.6 from 2,300 reviews", not "loved by thousands"),
- the founder showing how it's made,
- a guarantee, made visible.

## 6. One offer, one action

At the end, tell the viewer exactly one thing to do: "Shop the starter kit", "Try it for ₹299", "Get 20% off your first order". Two offers split attention. No offer leaves the viewer to work it out, and they won't.

## 7. Brand it so it's recognised with the sound off

People remember brands through distinctive, repeated cues: a colour, a shape, a sound, a character, a way of filming. Put yours in the first seconds and keep them the same across every ad. Consistency across a hundred ads builds more recognition than brilliance in one.

The theory behind this is worth knowing, and it's covered in [Propaganda, Persuasion and Your Brand](/blog/brand-perception-propaganda-theory).

## 8. Make it for the placement

A Reel, a YouTube pre-roll and a product page video are three different ads:

- **Reels and Shorts:** vertical, hook in two seconds, captions burned in, 15–30 seconds.
- **YouTube pre-roll:** the first five seconds must work before "Skip".
- **Product page:** no hook needed, since they're already interested. Show detail and use.

## 9. Make many, then let the numbers decide

No one knows in advance which hook will win. The brands that win make several versions, with three hooks on the same body or two offers on the same story, then spend behind the winner.

This used to be too expensive for small brands. With AI-assisted production, a creator can make five hook variations for little more than the cost of one.

## The fundamentals checklist

- One job, in one sentence.
- One buyer, in one moment.
- A hook that works in two seconds with the sound off.
- Product truth shown, not claimed.
- Proof that lowers the risk.
- One offer, one action.
- Distinctive brand cues, repeated in every ad.
- Cut for the placement.
- Several variations, tested.
